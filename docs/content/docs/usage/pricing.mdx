> This describes the **hosted API** at `api.barrelman.dev`. A self-hosted
> instance meters usage the same way but has nothing to charge — subscription
> billing is gated on a license only the official deployment holds, because the
> Commons Clause forbids selling barrelman. See [LICENSING.md](https://github.com/alexwohlbruck/barrelman/blob/main/LICENSING.md).

Usage is metered in **credits**, not requests, because barrelman's endpoints are
wildly unequal. A vector tile is one indexed read; an isochrone fans out to
hundreds of GraphHopper calls. Charging both as "one request" would either give
tiles away at a loss or price routing as if it were free.

Credits are integers with the cheapest operation as the unit, so a balance stays
exact across millions of small charges — no floating-point drift.

## What things cost

| Group | Credits | Endpoints |
|---|---|---|
| `tiles` | 1 per source | `/tiles/*`, `/styles/*` |
| `places` | 3 | `/place/*`, `/brands` |
| `spatial` | 3 | `/contains`, `/children` |
| `geocode` | 5 | `/geocode/*` |
| `search` | 6 | `/search`, `/autocomplete` |
| `routing` | 12 | `/route`, `/route/*`, `/match` (per 100 points), `/graphhopper/route` |
| `transit` | 20 | `/transit/*`, `/gbfs/*` |
| `isochrone` | 40 per contour | `/isochrone` |

Three kinds of request are priced by how much they ask for, because one of
them can carry many times the work of another:

- **Tiles** cost 1 credit per source the tile reads. A bundle such as `detail`
  or a comma-joined composite such as `buildings_3d,parking_areas` costs one
  credit per member, the same as fetching each source on its own. Bundling
  still saves round trips and latency. TileJSON (`/tiles/<source>.json`) and
  map styles (`/styles/*`) are free.
- **`/isochrone`** costs 40 credits per contour. `durations=300,600,900` is
  three contours, 120 credits. With no `durations` it computes one 15-minute
  contour. `/isochrone/modes` is free.
- **`/match`** costs 12 credits per started hundred points: up to 100 points is
  12, 101 to 200 is 24, and the 2,000-point maximum is 240.

`X-Barrelman-Credits-Charged` on each response gives the exact amount. A request
refused before it reaches the engine (a malformed tile source, too many
contours, a trace over the point limit) is charged one unit.

The `/graphhopper` passthrough forwards `route`, `info` and `health` only. Use
`/isochrone` and `/match` for reachability and map matching.

The ratios are calibrated against the market rather than invented. Taking a tile
as 1, [Mapbox](https://www.mapbox.com/pricing) prices geocoding at roughly 3×
and directions at 8×; [Stadia Maps](https://stadiamaps.com/pricing/) prices both
at 20×. Barrelman sits between them, which is roughly where the real cost sits
too — a geocode is more than a tile read but nowhere near a routing solve.

A test pins that ratio inside the market band, so a future edit cannot quietly
drift out of it.

## Plans

| Plan | Price | Credits / month | Rate limit | Past the allowance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Free** | $0 | 100,000 | 300 / min | **Stops with `402`** |
| **Developer** | $19 | 1,000,000 | 900 / min | $0.030 / 1k |
| **Business** | $99 | 10,000,000 | 1,800 / min | $0.018 / 1k |
| **Scale** | $299 | 40,000,000 | 6,000 / min | $0.012 / 1k |
| **Enterprise** | Custom | Negotiated | Negotiated | $0.008 / 1k |

**Tiles get 20× that rate limit**, counted in a window of their own. The figures
above are shaped for API calls, one request and one answer; a map is not that
shape — a single viewport is thirty to sixty tiles, so a plan sized for
geocoding would make a basemap stutter after a few seconds of panning. Tiles are
also the cheapest thing on the list, 1 credit against 12–40, so the credit
allowance is already the honest budget for them. Free therefore draws 6,000
tiles a minute, Developer 18,000, Scale 120,000.

A tile burst spends no part of the budget the other endpoints are measured
against, and vice versa — that is the point of the separate window.

At Developer, $19 buys 200,000 geocodes, 166,000 searches or 83,000 routes. The
same geocoding volume on Mapbox is roughly $150.

Free is **evaluation and non-commercial**; every paid plan includes commercial
use. That matches how MapTiler and Stadia license theirs.

Enterprise is `contactOnly` — checkout refuses it with a "get in touch" rather
than sending anyone to a payment page.

### The plans that are not on the ladder

Two, `demo` and `first-party`, which do not appear here, in `/account/plans`, or
in the console's plan picker, because they cannot be bought. Both are
`internal: true` and assigned by an administrator.

`first-party` is for an application the operator runs themselves — Parchment, in
our case. It is unmetered *and* unthrottled: metering your own product means
billing yourself, and throttling it means rationing your own users. See
[abuse controls](/self-hosting/abuse-controls) for why that is a plan rather
than an exemption on the shared service key.

`demo` serves the API **unmetered** too, and is what runs the interactive demo
on the landing page.

A demo cannot be a normal account: metered, it eventually stops, and it stops on
whichever afternoon the page gets attention. What bounds it instead is a
per-visitor rate limit, 240/min per address inside a 3,000/min account ceiling
(4,800 and 60,000 for tiles). That is a better fit for the actual risk — one account, thousands of strangers,
where an account-wide limit means the first scraper decides everyone else's
experience.

Unmetered is not unbilled-and-unseen: usage is still recorded at zero credits,
so demo traffic shows up in the dashboards and in abuse detection like anyone
else's. See
[accounts & API keys](/usage/accounts#unmetered-keys-and-why-nobody-can-mint-one) for why
this is a plan rather than a kind of key.

### Two deliberate properties

**The free tier stops rather than billing.** At zero credits the API answers
`402` with the reset date until the next cycle:

```json
{
  "error": "Credit allowance exhausted for this billing period. …",
  "code": "insufficient_credits",
  "remaining": 0,
  "resetsAt": "2026-09-01T00:00:00.000Z"
}
```

Nobody can run up a bill on a plan they did not pay for.

**Overage is capped.** Paid plans keep serving past the allowance, but only up
to a multiple of it (3x on Developer and Business, 2x on Scale); past that,
requests are refused with a `402` carrying `code: "overage_limit_reached"`. A
leaked key would otherwise accrue charges with no ceiling — a Developer key at
its own rate limit against `/isochrone` is roughly $1/minute — and the
burn-rate detector only runs on the hourly sweep. Enterprise is uncapped
because its volume is contractual.

**Overage stays close to the included rate** — 1.6–1.8×, not the 10× an earlier
draft charged. The customer hitting overage is the one about to buy the next
plan up; punishing them for it is backwards. A test asserts this stays within 2×,
and that a bigger plan is never more expensive per credit.

## Cycles and balances

On a paid plan the cycle follows your subscription: if you subscribed on the
14th, your allowance resets on the 14th of each month, the day you are billed.
When a month has no such day (the 31st in February), the cycle resets on that
month's last day. The Free plan resets on the 1st of each calendar month. Both
reset at midnight UTC, and the allowance does not roll over.

Changing plan mid-cycle splits the cycle at the day of the change, and each
part includes its plan's allowance in proportion to the days it covers, the way
the price is prorated. Developer for 9 days of a 30-day cycle and then Business
for 21 includes 300,000 + 7,000,000 credits. Usage before the change is judged
against the old plan's share and any overage on it is billed at the change, so
the new plan only judges what comes after. Together the parts never include
more than one allowance of the biggest plan you held that cycle.

A new subscription starts a new cycle on the day it begins, with a whole
allowance. The Free plan is not prorated.
`GET /account/credits` returns the current cycle's `cycleStartedAt` and
`cycleResetsAt`.

A balance has two parts:

1. The plan's monthly allowance.
2. Credits from packs and grants, which never expire.

The allowance is spent **first**. Pack credits are only touched once the
allowance runs out, so a pack survives a month that stayed inside it.

A pack is used up as you spend it. When a cycle closes, whatever it took from
your packs is recorded in your credit history as a negative entry, and the next
cycle starts with what is left. A pack bought after a cycle has closed is never
applied to that cycle, and credits already billed as overage are not also taken
from a pack.

Packs can be bought on any plan, Free included. On Free a pack raises the hard
stop: requests continue past the allowance until the pack runs out, and then
stop with a `402`. On a paid plan, overage is billed only once the pack is
empty.

## Changing or ending a subscription

An account has one subscription. Switching plans moves that subscription onto
the new plan rather than opening a second one. An upgrade takes effect at once
and the prorated difference for the rest of the period is charged straight
away. A downgrade also takes effect at once, and the unused part of the period
is credited to the next invoice. Enterprise is arranged directly and cannot be
switched to from the console.

Cancelling keeps the plan until the end of the period already paid for. The
console shows "Cancels on" with the date, and the account moves to the free plan
when the period ends. Cancelling can be undone from the billing portal until
then.

If a renewal payment fails, the plan stays on while the payment provider
retries it. The console shows a banner linking to the billing portal, and one
email goes out when the payment first fails. The email is not resent if it
cannot be delivered, so the banner is the notice to rely on. If the retries also fail, the
subscription ends and the account moves to the free plan.

A refunded credit pack takes its credits back, in proportion to the amount
refunded. Credits from the pack that were already spent count against the
balance like any other usage: on a plan with overage they are billed as
overage. A refunded subscription payment ends the plan only if the subscription
was ended along with the refund.

Deleting an account ends its subscription immediately and bills any overage
owed up to that point.

## Headers

| Header | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `X-Barrelman-Credits-Charged` | What this request cost |
| `X-Barrelman-Overage` | Present when the charge fell past the allowance |
| `Retry-After` | On a `429`, seconds until the window rolls |

A request that fails with a **5xx is refunded** — customers should not pay for
our outages.

## How metering works

Barrelman serves autocomplete: one request per keystroke. A database write per
request would cost more than the read it is measuring. So counters accumulate in
memory and flush on a timer as a single multi-row upsert, keyed by
(user, key, day, endpoint group).

The trade-off is explicit: a crash loses at most one flush interval of counters,
always in the customer's favour. **Credit grants are never buffered** — those go
straight to the ledger.

Quota decisions read a short-lived cache rather than the database, so an account
can overshoot by at most the traffic it lands inside one TTL. Bounded, small,
and much cheaper than a round trip per request.

Neither of those applies to money coming *in*: purchases are written
synchronously and keyed on the provider's order ID.

## Where the numbers live

`src/billing/plans.ts` is the source of truth. `GET /account/plans` serves it.

The marketing site duplicates the figures so it can build statically with no API
dependency. If the two ever disagree, `/account/plans` is authoritative.

Existing subscribers keep the price they signed up at until you change it with
the payment provider. The *allowance* changes for everyone straight away,
because it is read from the code on every request.
